My friend built Skolekartet, a map of every school in Norway and its test results. She asked me to help with a real case: a parent with MS choosing a school for their kids. 01 · The problem

I used the site the way one family would. It couldn’t do the job.

Picture it. I’m sitting next to a friend who has MS. We’re looking for a school for their child. When we find one, the parent needs to see why that school is right.

Skolekartet had a strong start. It was clean and honest about its data. But for us, it got stuck in four places.

  • “Best” meant test scores. The colours, the filters and the sorting all pointed there. Wellbeing was a bare number, and access wasn’t there at all.
  • No answer to “can my child get in?” Nothing about a step-free entrance, a lift or extra support. Not even a note saying the facts were missing.
  • Numbers without meaning. A card said “Bullying 26,1 %”. Is that bad? You had to work out by hand that it was more than double Oslo’s figure.
  • The list stayed on one computer. No way to compare, share or print. The parent couldn’t look at it on their own phone, with their own settings.
02 · A surprise

Then I noticed who I had left out.

My first ideas were all about access. Test results sat at the very bottom, as an extra.

That was my bias, not the parents’. Most families choose a school by its results and by how happy the pupils are. The family with MS cares about that too, right next to a step-free entrance.

So the question changed. Not “which school is best?” but “which school fits our child?” And every family gets to answer it for themselves.

To keep myself honest, I wrote down the parents the site has to work for. They’re made-up people, built from the review and from how school choice works in Norway, so they still need real interviews. But each one pulled the design in a new direction.

03 · The big change

Every family says what matters. Every number comes with words.

First, one question

Results count as much as access

After the address, the site asks one thing: what matters to your family? Learning and wellbeing come first. Then getting around, support and everyday life.

Nothing is ticked from the start, so the site never decides for you. I ask about needs, not diagnoses, and choices about the child are never stored on a server.

Each card says how well the school fits, in words

Before, a card showed bare numbers and a guessed school district that looked certain. Now it tells you how many of your priorities a school meets. Results, wellbeing and bullying sit next to the national average, in words: above, the same or below.

Why not one big score? Because a parent can’t check a single score, or explain it to someone else. A list of what fits, they can.

On the map

The legend is a filter, and every pin explains itself

Parents choose what the map shows: fit, results, wellbeing or bullying. Tap a colour in the legend to hide or show those schools. Tap a pin, and it tells you in words what its colour means.

Everything on the map is also in the list next to it. Nobody has to see the map to use the site.

The school page answers the hard question first

Before, the school page was a long run of tables and charts, with nothing about access. Now it opens with “How the school fits you”: the numbers in words, what’s confirmed, what’s still unknown, and questions to ask the school, right next to a call button.

Decide together

A shortlist that travels to the parent’s own phone

Saved schools turn into a real comparison. It starts with plain answers, like “Majorstuen stands out on bullying” or “Step-free access confirmed at all three”. “Best of these” only shows up where a difference is real.

Someone asked me: without an account, won’t the list disappear? But families use the site a few times, years apart. An account is one more password, holding data about a child. So “Send the list to myself” texts or e-mails a private link instead. It opens on any phone, in large text, with read-aloud.

04 · Why Designsystemet

I built it on the design system Norway’s public services already share.

Designsystemet is the open design system behind Norwegian public services. A school finder for parents is that kind of service, so the choice was easy.

  • It feels familiar. Parents already use NAV, Skatteetaten and their kommune. The school finder looks and behaves the same way.
  • Accessibility comes built in. The parts already handle keyboard use, contrast and screen readers. That matters most for parents who use a screen reader or very large text.
  • My friend can build it straight away. Her site already runs on Designsystemet, so every part of my design is a part she already has. Dark mode and high contrast come with it.
  • Less to look after. Skolekartet is a side project. Fixes and updates come from the system, not from one busy person.

I only made my own pieces where the system had nothing: the coloured edge on each school card, the number tiles and the map pins. Even those use the system’s colours and spacing, so they fit right in.

05 · Breaking it

Then I tried to break it.

I walked each parent through the design again and poked at the weak spots. What if the internet is slow? What if no school fits? What if the text is huge, the screen is dark, or the parent can’t see the map at all?

Every answer became a new screen: results and the map on a phone, high contrast, a calmer dark theme, a page to print for the grandparent who likes paper, and a start page in Ukrainian for a family new to Norway.

06 · What’s next

Next, real parents get to try it.

The working prototype is ready. I’ll sit with parents on a laptop and on a phone, with their own screen readers and settings, and give them one situation:

“You’ve just moved to Majorstuen in Oslo. Your child uses a wheelchair and needs extra support at school. Find a school that fits, and tell me why.”

Then I’ll watch. Where do they stop? Do they read “less bullying” the right way round? Can they find their list again a week later, on another phone? I’ll invite parents who care most about results, parents with disabilities, parents new to Norway and a grandparent or two.

Some things are honest placeholders for now. Test results and access facts are example data until schools confirm them, and the design has a page where schools can do just that. The translations and the texts about parents’ rights still need an expert to check them.

How I’ll know it works

Sign 01

A shortlist, sooner

Parents get to a short list of schools faster, and open fewer school pages on the way.

Sign 02

Facts read the right way

Parents can tell what’s confirmed and what’s still unknown, without a legend to help them.

Sign 03

It works on the parent’s own phone

Shared lists get opened on another device, and screen-reader users finish on their own.

What I’d tell myself at the start

Lesson 01

Look for who’s missing

My first idea served the family I started with and forgot the biggest group. Going back to look for who I’d left out changed the whole design.

Lesson 02

A number needs words

A percentage on its own is a puzzle. “Less bullying than the national average” is an answer a tired parent can use.

Lesson 03

Say what you don’t know

An empty space looks like “no problem”. “Unknown, ask the school” is more honest, and more useful.
