Helping a friend · 2026 · in progress

The school map showed test scores. I made it show which school fits your child.

My friend built Skolekartet, a map of every school in Norway. I sat down with a parent who has MS to find a school for their child. We got stuck four times. This is how I fixed it.

Challenge

Parents saw numbers, not answers. And nothing told a family if their child could even get through the door.

Solution

Each family says what matters. Every number comes with words. The shortlist opens on any phone, no account needed.

My role

Helping a friend: review, sketches, writing, design in Figma and a working prototype.

Status

Prototype ready. Testing with real parents is next.

The story in 30 seconds
  1. The problem “Bullying 26,1 %.” Is that bad? The site showed numbers with no meaning, and nothing about access.
  2. The twist My first fix was biased too. I put access first and hid test results. Most parents start with results.
  3. The result “Which school fits us?” Not “which is best”. Every family answers it for themselves.

01 · Stuck

One parent, one evening, four dead ends.

We weren’t looking for the “best” school. We needed a school where the child could get in, feel safe and learn. And the parent needed to see why.

  • “Best” meant test scores Colours, filters and sorting all pointed there.
  • Numbers without meaning 26,1 % bullying is more than double Oslo’s figure. You had to work that out yourself.
  • No answer to “can my child get in?” No step-free entrance, no lift, no support. Not even a note that it was missing.
  • The list stayed on one computer No way to share it with the parent’s own phone.
02 · My own bias

My first sketch had the same problem, just upside down.

I was so focused on access that I pushed test results to the very bottom, as an extra.

That was my bias, not the parents’. Most families choose by results and by how happy the pupils are. The family with MS cares about that too.

So I changed the question the whole site answers:

Which school is best?
→
Which school fits our child?

To keep myself honest, I wrote down four parents the site has to work for. Each one pulled the design a different way.

“Show me the results. But tell me if the difference is real.”

Henrik · chooses by test results

“I need a school where Lina gets through the door.”

Amina · her daughter uses a wheelchair

“Does the next school actually do something about bullying?”

Sara · her son is being bullied

“If the map is the only thing that works, it isn’t made for me.”

Lars · blind, uses a screen reader

These are made-up parents, built from the review and how school choice works in Norway. Real interviews come next.

03 · Five decisions

From paper to a working site, one decision at a time.

Decision 1

Ask what matters before showing a single school.

Two short questions: where do you live, and what matters to you? Learning and wellbeing come first, then getting around, support and everyday life.

why?

Nothing is ticked from the start, so the site never decides for you. I ask about needs, not diagnoses, and choices about the child never leave the browser.

Decision 2

Every number comes with words.

Each card says how many of your priorities a school fits. Results, wellbeing and bullying say above, the same or below average. What we don’t know says “ask the school”.

How to read the sketch
  • Colour edge. Green, orange or red shows how well a school fits you, before you read a word.
  • “2 av 3”. Fit is counted in your own priorities, not turned into a score.
  • Dashed box. Say what we don’t know, instead of leaving it out.
  • My note. Parents should understand the card without the legend.
why?

Why not one big score? A parent can’t check a score, or explain it to their partner. A list of what fits, they can.

Decision 3

The legend is the filter.

Parents pick what the map shows: fit, results, wellbeing or bullying. Tap a colour to hide those schools. Everything on the map is also in the list next to it.

why?

One less thing to learn. And nobody has to see the map to use the site, which matters for Lars.

Decision 4

The school page answers the hard question first.

Before: tables and charts, nothing about access. After: “How the school fits you” at the top, with what’s confirmed, what’s unknown, and questions to ask, right next to a call button.

Decision 5

The list travels. There’s no account.

Save a few schools, compare them, then send one link. It opens on the parent’s own phone, in large text, in their language, or read aloud.

→
why?

Families use the site a few times, years apart. An account means one more password to remember, and personal details about a child stored somewhere. A private link does the same job.

04 · Why Designsystemet

Built on the design system Norway’s public services already share.

  • It feels familiar Parents already use NAV, Skatteetaten and their kommune.
  • Accessible from the start Keyboard, contrast and screen readers are handled.
  • My friend can build it Her site already runs on it, so every part already exists.
  • Less to look after It’s a side project. Fixes come from the system.
05 · Breaking it

Then I tried to break it.

I walked each parent through the design again and asked mean questions:

  • What if the internet is slow?
  • What if no school fits?
  • What if the text is huge?
  • What if the screen is dark?
  • What if you can’t see the map?
  • What if grandma wants paper?

Every answer became a screen.

06 · What’s next

Next, real parents get to try it.

I’ll sit with parents on their own laptop and phone, with their own settings, and give them one task:

“You’ve just moved to Majorstuen. Your child uses a wheelchair and needs extra support. Find a school that fits, and tell me why.”

Then I’ll watch. Where do they stop? Do they read “less bullying” the right way round? Can they find their list a week later, on another phone?

How I’ll know it works

Sign 01

A shortlist, sooner

Parents reach a short list faster, opening fewer school pages on the way.

Sign 02

Facts read the right way

Parents can tell confirmed from unknown, without a legend.

Sign 03

It works on their own phone

Shared lists open on another device, and screen-reader users finish on their own.

What I’d tell myself at the start

Lesson 01

Look for who’s missing

My first idea helped the family I started with and forgot the biggest group.

Lesson 02

A number needs words

“Less bullying than average” is an answer a tired parent can use. A percentage is a puzzle.

Lesson 03

Say what you don’t know

An empty space looks like “no problem”. “Unknown, ask the school” is honest and useful.

Honest placeholders. Access facts are example data until schools confirm them, and there’s a page where schools can do just that. The translations and the texts about parents’ rights still need an expert check.
