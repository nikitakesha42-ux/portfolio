Helping a friend · 2026 · in progress

Redesigning Skolekartet so every family can find what matters to them.

My friend built Skolekartet: national test results for every school in Norway, on a map. It’s clean, honest about its data and built on Designsystemet. She asked me to help make it better, for a very real situation: a parent with MS choosing a school for their kids. I used it the way that family would, and it couldn’t do the job. Then I looked wider: some parents choose by test results, some by wellbeing and bullying, some by access and support, and most by a mix. So I redesigned it to let every family say what matters, and to explain all of it in plain words. It’s heading into testing with parents.

01 · The review

I used the site the way one family would. It couldn’t do the job.

The lens: I’m a friend of a parent with a disability such as MS. Together we need to find the right school for their child, and when I show the parent the result, it has to be obvious why that school. I reviewed all 17 screens of skolekartet.no that way, with numbered pins on each screen and a notes panel beside it.

The first version has a strong base. But for this family it defines “best” as test averages, says nothing about access or support, depends on a map and colours that aren’t accessible, and gives no way to compare, share or explain a choice.

Nine issues that break the whole journey

  1. 01“Best” means test scores. Colours, filters, sorting and the main button all revolve around them.
  2. 02Nothing about step-free access, lifts, accessible toilets or special education. Not on cards, not on school pages.
  3. 03No way to explain “why this school”. Raw numbers and red and green dots instead of reasons.
  4. 04Saved schools live in one browser. No comparing, sharing or printing, so the parent can’t see them on their own device.
  5. 05Map and colour first. Hard with a screen reader, low contrast in places, and red and green unsafe for colour-blind users.
  6. 06A meaningless default view: random schools in Rana before you’ve said where you live.
  7. 07Guidance for parents hidden in a menu, and no page about rights, PPT, IEPs or school transport.
  8. 08Distances that assume a child walking at 4.5 km/h. No step-free or wheelchair route.
  9. 09One contact channel, and it’s a written form. Parents who can’t write easily are left out.

When I checked the live site again on 5 November 2026, some of this had moved: the likely school district now comes first with walking time, the filter button shows a live count, and there are three languages and new guides for parents. Most of the issues above were still open.

02 · Research

From what families do, to what they need.

I broke the problem into eleven concrete behaviours, each with its trigger, what the family relies on, where the site falls short and the workaround they use instead. Then I clustered them into four needs, ordered by how badly each one blocks the decision.

A second look: the parents I had left out

My first pass leaned on the family with the hardest access needs. When I went back through the behaviours, the largest group was missing: parents who choose by test results and by how pupils feel. Wanting good results isn’t a frame to correct. It’s a real need, and the site serves it badly too, with numbers that have nothing to compare against and colours without words.

Four needs, in priority order

Priority 1 · Blocks the decision

Clarity about access and support

Know before visiting whether the child can get in and around the school, and what support they’ll actually get. Today the site has no such data, and doesn’t say it’s missing.

Priority 2 · Excludes the decision-maker

A decision the parent shares

The parent, who has a disability, needs the same shortlist on their own device and assistive technology, and to understand why each school is on it.

Priority 3 · Risk of a wrong choice

Things worth checking

Spot at once the facts that should change the decision: high bullying, missing data, a guessed school district. Today every number looks equally certain.

Priority 4 · The most common question

Results and wellbeing you can read

Most parents look at test results, wellbeing and bullying first. They need them compared with the national average, in words, and told honestly when a difference is too small to mean anything or a child is exempt from the tests.

The question I chose to answer first

How might we reflect the child’s reality, their needs, access and support, in the school list rather than test-score averages, so the family feels informed instead of guessing?

One of 17 How Might We statements across the four needs, each tied to an observed behaviour and none with a solution built in. Three ideas answer it, each with a way to measure it:

  • A needs profile and a match line on every card. Three short questions, then every school says in words what fits and what’s unknown. Target: a three-school shortlist in half the time.
  • Known, unknown, ask. Every fact is Confirmed (with source and date), Unknown or Worth checking, with a named contact or a ready-made question. Target: 90 % answer “is this confirmed?” correctly.
  • Compare and share a “why” shortlist. A screen-reader-friendly comparison with one sentence per school, shared as a link without personal data or printed. Target: SUS of 80 or more with assistive-technology users.

A second question, for every parent

How might we show test results, wellbeing and bullying so every parent can read them correctly, without colour doing the work?

I answered it with a second round of ideas: three to five options side by side for each problem that kept coming back (the icons in the list, card height, the colours on the map, the filter and comparing schools), each with its reason, pros and cons. I kept nine and combined them with the needs-first ideas above, so one screen serves the parent who asks “are the results good?” and the parent who asks “can my child get in?”

  • Numbers in words. Test results, wellbeing and bullying as three tiles that say over, same as or under the national average, instead of bare numbers.
  • One colour, one meaning. A colour edge on the card replaces an icon whose shape meant school type and whose colour meant results, and it matches the pin on the map.
  • The legend is the filter. Colour buttons with a count each, on the map, and an explanation in words when you tap a pin.
  • Quick filters with counts, and answers before tables. You see how many schools are left before you tap, and a comparison opens with short answers per topic.

04 · Principles

Five principles, each traced to the issues it fixes.

  1. 01Every family defines “best”Results, wellbeing, access and distance are equal choices. The site never decides what matters for you, and every number is said in words.
  2. 02Say what we don’t knowEvery fact is Confirmed, Unknown or Worth checking, in words and icons. Silence is never read as “no problem”.
  3. 03List first, map optionalEverything on the map is also in the list, so nothing depends on seeing it.
  4. 04Why, in one sentenceEvery school says why it’s on the list, then gives the next step: call, ask or share.
  5. 05The parent stays in controlThe list goes to their own device, their own assistive technology, their own text size.

Why Designsystemet

Every screen uses the linked Designsystemet Figma library, not lookalike copies: real components, tokens and colour modes. For a public service like this, it was the obvious choice.

  • Made for exactly this kind of service. It is the shared, open-source design system for Norwegian public services, maintained by Digdir. A school finder for parents is a public-information service, so it should look and behave like the other public services they already use, such as NAV, Skatteetaten and their kommune.
  • Accessibility is built in, not added later. Universal design is required by law in Norway (likestillings- og diskrimineringsloven, WCAG 2.2). The components handle focus, keyboard use, contrast and screen-reader names. That matters most for this audience: parents who use JAWS, switch control or very large text.
  • The design maps straight to code. The live Skolekartet already runs on designsystemet-css, so every Button, Chip, Tag, Alert, Details, ToggleGroup, Search and Checkbox in Figma is a component my friend already has. No new UI library, no handoff gap.
  • Themes and colour modes for free. The green brand theme, the success/warning/neutral colours on the fact tags and the dark screen all come from Designsystemet’s tokens and its Light and Dark colour schemes. Dark mode is a switch, not a second design.
  • Less to maintain for a one-person project. Skolekartet is a side project. With shared components and open documentation, updates and accessibility fixes come from the system, not from my friend.

Only three things are custom: the school tile with its coloured edge, the three number tiles and the map pins. Each is built from Designsystemet tokens, so it matches the components around it. Every redesigned screen also has a rationale panel beside it: what was wrong, how I fixed it and why, what it does for accessibility, and what I considered and rejected.

05 · The redesign

21 screens, one journey: where you live, what matters, then the schools that fit.

Step 2 · What matters to you?

Every family defines “best”, and results count as much as access

After one question about where they live, grouped checkboxes in everyday language, each with a short description. Learning and wellbeing come first, as equals: good results on the national tests, pupils who thrive, little bullying. Then getting around, support and everyday life. Each option says what it changes, and nothing is selected by default.

In the first version, results sat at the bottom as an optional extra. That was my bias, not the parents’. Most families care about results and wellbeing, and the family with MS cares about them too, next to step-free access. I asked about needs, not diagnoses, and choices about the child are never stored on a server.

Step 3 · The results

Every card answers “does this school work for us?” and says the numbers in words

Sorted by fit, then distance. Each card says “Fits 3 of 4 priorities”, then shows three tiles for test results, wellbeing and bullying, each compared with the national average in words: over, same as or under. Below them come the access facts with their status, the walking time and the step-free route. The guessed school district is labelled as a guess, with a link to the kommune.

Quick filters above the list show how many schools are left before you tap, and a thin colour edge on each card matches the school’s pin on the map. I rejected a single “match %” and a single score. A count of priorities met, plus the numbers in words, is easier to check and to explain to someone else. Help panels, like the school-district note and the card legend, start closed, so the list comes first.

Step 3 · On the map

The legend is a filter, and every pin explains itself

Parents choose what the map shows: fit, results, wellbeing or bullying. The legend becomes buttons with a count per colour that also hide or show groups, and a pin opens a short explanation of its colour in words, with the school’s other numbers and facts.

Everything on the map is still in the list beside it, in the same order, so nobody depends on seeing the map. The legend can be hidden and the explanation closed with ✕, so the map never drowns in boxes.

Step 4 · The school page

The hard question first, the details after

“How the school fits you” opens the page: the match, the three numbers in words, the access facts with status, questions to ask the school, a call button and a copy button for the questions. Below it, a new table covers entrance, lift, toilet, adapted teaching, special groups, after-school care and quiet rooms, each with source and date.

The route is shown for a wheelchair, on foot and by public transport, and written out as text for anyone who can’t read a map. Bullying and wellbeing are sentences with the comparison in words. Long sections, like the access table and the route map, start short and open on request.

Step 5 · Decide together

A shortlist that travels to the parent’s own phone

Saved schools become a real comparison. It opens with short answers for everything the family chose, and for results, wellbeing and bullying either way: “Marienlyst slightly higher, but two or three points can be chance”, “Majorstuen stands out on bullying”, “Step-free access confirmed at all three”. Below, the schools sit side by side with the same tiles, and “Best of these” only appears where a difference is real. The full table is one tap away, closed by default. Share it as a link with no personal data, by SMS or e-mail, or print it.

On the parent’s phone the list opens in large text, with a text-size control, read-aloud, three languages and a call button on every school. A reviewer pointed out that without an account, everything disappears. But families use the site a few times, often years apart, and an account is one more password to forget, holding data about a child. So the list stays in the browser, and “Send the list to myself” e-mails or texts a private link that brings it back on any device. We don’t store the address, and “Add the deadlines to your calendar” brings people back when it matters.

06 · Before and after

Six screens, today and redesigned.

From random schools in Rana to one clear question

Today the start page ranks ten schools in Rana as “nearest” before you’ve said where you live, and the address field has no visible label. The redesign asks one question, “Where do you live?”, with a visible label and help text, and shows no list until there’s an answer.

From bare numbers to fit, in words

Today the cards show averages, wellbeing and bullying as bare numbers, a guessed school district that looks certain, and a map that takes two thirds of the screen. The redesign leads with the list, says how well each school fits, puts the same three numbers into words against the national average, and labels every fact and every estimate.

From score filters to needs

Today’s filter starts with score bands, then school type and ownership, with every box pre-checked, so ticking one seems to do nothing. A live count on the button helps, but the first question is still about test scores. The redesign starts with learning and wellbeing, then accessibility and support. Nothing is selected, and every explanation stays closed until you ask for it.

From 5 900 pixels of tables to an answer

Today’s school page is a long run of tables and charts with no summary and nothing about access. The redesign answers “how does this school do, and can my child get in and be supported here?” first, then gives the next step.

From a list stuck in one browser to a shared decision

Today saved schools live in one browser, and the empty state points to an icon it doesn’t show. The redesign answers “what sets them apart?” first, for every topic, then compares the shortlist row by row and sends it anywhere: link, SMS, e-mail or paper.

From standard deviations to plain words

Today “Om fargefordelingen” explains the colours with scale points, intervals and standard deviation. Its replacement, “Slik leser du tallene”, starts with four short truths: results are averages for a whole group, a difference of one or two points is uncertain, many pupils with adapted teaching are exempt, and every colour comes with words.

07 · Stress test

Then I tried to break it.

I walked every persona and journey through the redesign again, and checked each screen for states (empty, loading, error, success, permissions), devices (phone, desktop, print), settings (200 to 400 % zoom, high contrast, dark theme, reduced motion), assistive technology, languages, slow networks and the map. It found twelve gaps. Eighteen new screens close them.

Two more rounds on the map markers

Design review found that same-coloured shapes were hard to tell apart, and that coloured number badges made markers harder to read. The fifth version follows WCAG: one neutral style, a white number on a near-black disc, a type icon with text, the school name and its match on every marker, and distinct selected and focus states. In the combined version the parent can switch the map to results, wellbeing or bullying; then the number disc takes the colour, and the legend and the pin explain it in words.

08 · Testing plan

One scenario, ten tasks, and parents doing them in their own way.

The working prototype is ready. This is the session I’ll run with it, on a laptop and on a phone, with people using their own assistive technology where they have it.

“You’ve moved to Kirkeveien 64A in Oslo. Your child uses a wheelchair and needs adapted teaching.”

  1. Find the schools that fit your child best. Which would you look at more closely, and why?
  2. Find them on the map. Which is closest, and how well does it fit?
  3. Find out whether Majorstuen school has a lift, and what you should ask the school.
  4. Save three schools and send the list to someone in your family.
  5. The text is too small for you. Make it bigger.
  6. You want to report wrong information. How do you do it?
  7. On a phone, do tasks 1 and 3 again.
  8. Your child loves maths. Which nearby school has the best test results, and is the difference real?
  9. Of your three saved schools, where do pupils thrive most and where is bullying lowest?
  10. Close the browser. A week later, on another phone, find your list again.

I’ll watch whether people understand “3/4” and the status words without the legend, whether they can tell Confirmed, Unknown and Worth checking apart, and where they hesitate or go back. I’ll note time per task, misclicks, and how sure they are of each answer from 1 to 5. I’ll also check that parents read the tiles right, especially that lower bullying is better. Participants: parents who choose mainly by results or wellbeing, parents with and without disabilities, parents new to Norway, grandparents, and professionals from PPT and schools.

09 · What’s next

A working prototype, then testing with real families.

The prototype is built and ready for the first round of testing with parents. Before anything goes live, four things are still open, and both the design file and the prototype are honest about all of them.

  • Usability testing with 8 to 10 people, including screen-reader, keyboard-only and low-vision users, and parents with limited Norwegian.
  • Real data. Access and support facts and contact details in the redesign are illustrative placeholders. Bullying, wellbeing, results and distances come from skolekartet.no. A new screen lets schools confirm their own facts, which is how the placeholders become real.
  • Human review of the translations, and a legal review of the rights texts against the Education Act.
  • Map performance and clustering at kommune level, and low-data behaviour on real devices.
