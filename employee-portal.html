01 · the problem

The problem hiring managers don't always hear

Most equity tools are designed for the people who work with equity every day — finance, HR, plan admins. The portal was being used by everyone else: regular employees who log in twice a year and need to remember everything from scratch each time.

The result was a portal that explained equity correctly, but didn't help people act on it. Tickets to Customer Success were mostly the same shape: "I see this number, what does it mean for me?" Trust in compensation slowly eroded, even when nothing had actually changed.

02 · framing

How I framed it

I treated this as a re-engagement problem, not a finance UI problem. Every interaction had to assume the user had forgotten the previous one. The dashboard had to answer two questions before anything else: what do I have right now, and what should I do next?

"If a piece of the UI doesn't help with what do I have or what should I do, it doesn't earn its space." — the rule I held every later decision to
03 · design decisions

Four design decisions that carried the story

design decision 01 · systems thinking

A dashboard built around what the user holds, not what we can store

The old dashboard assumed everyone held a single instrument type. As soon as employees had a mix — options, RSUs, shares, savings — the page got crowded and the totals stopped meaning the same thing for everyone.

In research, three questions kept showing up: how does this change over time, what plan does it belong to, what kind of equity is it? Those questions became the three views — Per Year, Per Plan, Per Instrument — instead of a single chart trying to mean four things at once.

learning 01 One chart trying to answer four questions teaches users nothing about any of them — three small, sharp views always beat one general one.
learning 02 People don't ask for a "dashboard" — they ask "what do I have". The angle the user enters with becomes the layout's anchor.
design decision 02 · creative direction

The simulator, treated as a tool — not a flourish

The earlier simulator was a hover-state on the share price card — easy to miss, easy to misread. Employees in testing read it as marketing dressing rather than something they could act on.

I made it its own destination with an explicit "Simulate" button on the dashboard, and gave it the controls people actually wanted: a price slider, a time horizon, and a side-by-side comparison against their real equity. The reason for the move was simple — if a feature is the answer to "what could this be worth", it deserves a room, not a corner.

design decision 03 · storytelling

Action flows that read like a conversation

Exercising, selling, accepting an award and choosing a savings rate are rare but consequential. The previous flows were technically correct but emotionally tense: dense forms, internal jargon, confirm buttons that didn't quite say what you were committing to.

I rebuilt each one as a short, ordered conversation: one decision per step, the consequence visible inline, and the user's own language — "purchase share", not "subscribe to issuance". The last screen always restates what's about to happen. There are no irreversible last clicks.

design decision 04 · systems thinking

Education that lives next to the thing it explains

Equity is a learning curve and a localisation problem at the same time — every country has different terminology, every employer wants their own voice. Instead of static help articles, education sits inside the product: tooltips at the term, examples at the calculation, deeper reading one step away when the user wants it.

A separate wording system lets clients adjust tone, labels and explanations without designers in the loop. Brand theming adopts the client's primary colours while keeping the clarity and accessibility of the base system intact. The two pieces are deliberately decoupled — a client can change voice without breaking accessibility, and vice versa.

04 · research

The insight the whole dashboard was built on

Research wasn't one big study — it was a steady drip of small ones: card sorting, prototype tests on desktop and mobile, moderated and unmoderated sessions, a Lean UX canvas to sanity-check assumptions, and the in-portal feedback widget that ran continuously. Most of it confirmed what the team already suspected. One pattern, though, kept showing up, and it changed how I designed everything else.

the insight

People open the portal infrequently — and almost every session begins with the same two questions: what do I have right now, and what should I do next?

surfaced first in card sorting, confirmed in seven follow-up prototype tests
01 Participants grouped "tax" under "when I cash out", not under "how it works". Tax stopped being a help-article topic and became a step inside the action flows.
02 "Vesting" was the only term consistently understood across roles — every other equity word needed a tooltip. The glossary became a design system component.
03 Instrument types clustered tightly — participants grouped "options" with "options" regardless of plan. That justified the Per Instrument view on the dashboard.
04 A third of participants gave up before sorting more than twelve cards. That set a hard cap on how many top-level items the IA could ever expose.

The other studies are kept on file — happy to walk through any of them in a conversation, but they earned their place by confirming this insight, not by competing with it.

05 · opportunity

An opportunity I spotted: a direct line from users to designers

Most product teams hear from users through Customer Success, support tickets and quarterly NPS — a long chain with a lot of filtering. I argued the portal could host its own listening tool. A small in-product feedback widget, owned by design, with replies routed to the same place we already wrote tickets.

It was a small build with an outsized payoff. Inside two months we had qualitative data we couldn't get any other way — at the moment of an action, in the user's own words, in Norwegian and English.

06 · metrics

Reading the feedback trend in Mixpanel

The chart below tracks the widget's three reactions — happy, neutral, sad — month by month. Two things stood out, and both shaped the next quarter of work.

First: positive responses ran ahead of negative ones almost every month — but the gap was thin until each new release. Releases were the engine of sentiment, not generic time. That told me the design changes were doing the work; the dashboard wasn't drifting upward on its own.

Second: every dip in the happy line lined up with a specific friction we already knew about — the simulator entry point, the share-price card, the equity-action flow. The chart wasn't surfacing new problems; it was confirming the order we should fix the known ones in. That confirmation is what made the case for continuing the redesign in chunks.

07 · business judgment

When implementation hit pause: protecting the biggest client at risk

One of the dashboard's biggest unresolved frictions was an edge case that mattered to a single client. Not just any client — Optio's largest, with a contract that had been on a slow simmer of frustration for months. They'd been asking us to address the issue for a long time, and they had started talking like a customer with options.

I ran direct interviews and a round of testing with their participants, agreed on a direction with engineering, and we were ready to implement. Then the request landed: pause. Other projects had taken priority for the quarter.

That pause was the actual risk. Telling the biggest client "not this quarter either" would have ended a multi-year relationship over a problem they'd already explained three times.

I worked with our Product Owner to break the implementation into smaller, shippable slices and presented it at the quarterly OKR review. The proposal: ship the parts that solved the client's pain inside the upcoming releases we'd already committed to, defer the rest, keep the roadmap honest.

Leadership agreed. The new Dashboard rolled out in chunks across the following quarters. The client stayed.

08 · the shift

What shifted

The redesigned dashboard landed in one of the strongest months of positive feedback Optio had recorded for the portal. A later mobile app rollout was faster than expected — the IA and components were already responsive in intent.

Customer Success spends less time explaining basic concepts. The hardest tickets are now actually the hard ones. That's the shift this work was after.

09 · team

What it shifted for the team

A direct feedback widget, a tighter collaboration with Customer Success, and the chunking exercise during the pause all changed how the team made decisions together. The dashboard wasn't a designer's project anymore — it was the team's.

10 · what I'd carry forward

What I'd carry into the next consumer-finance product

  • Two questions are usually enough to anchor a dashboard. Adding a third often weakens the first two.
  • Plain language is a design system component. It has to be owned by the same people who own the colour tokens.
  • If a flow has an irreversible last click, it isn't designed yet — it's just built.
  • When the roadmap pauses, the designer's job is to find a smaller version of the same answer. A chunked plan, presented at OKR, can protect a client a deck full of complaints can't.
