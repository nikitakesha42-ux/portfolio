01 · The cracks

Built for one share type, asked to serve them all.

When I joined, the portal was still being built, mostly for stock options, and the company was in the middle of a big redesign. Then more share types, markets and company rules arrived, and the portal started to crack.

Risky actions felt unsafe. People couldn’t see what they actually owned. And we had almost no direct insight from the people using it.

My first move

Small on purpose: high impact, low cost. A quick fix went live first, and it bought time to gather insight for the real redesign.

03 · Two questions

Lots of small studies, one pattern.

Research wasn’t one big study. It was a steady drip of small ones. Most confirmed what the team suspected. One pattern changed everything: almost every visit started with “what do I have?” and “what should I do?”

The rule I designed against

If part of the screen doesn’t help with “what do I have” or “what should I do”, it doesn’t earn its space.

04 · Four decisions

Every decision answers one of the two questions.

● What do I have?

One dashboard that fits what you hold

Options, RSUs, PSUs, warrants or shares: the dashboard adapts to what each person actually holds, instead of pretending everything is an option.

● What should I do?

The simulator got its own page

It used to be a small widget for show. On its own page, with a slider, people could finally ask: what if the price goes up or down?

● What do I have?

Each company’s own words and colours

Clients can set their own wording, explanations and brand colours, while the portal keeps one shared design system. No one-off builds.

● Built unprompted

Ready for mobile before anyone asked

Mobile was low priority, but I used parts that adapt easily anyway. When the portal moved into the mobile app, the rollout shipped much faster.

05 · A direct line

I put a listening tool inside the product.

Most teams hear from users through Customer Success, support tickets and surveys. That’s a long chain with a lot of filtering. I argued for something shorter:

  1. Someone actsin the portal
  2. A small widget asksright next to the action
  3. Design reads itin their own words, NO or EN
  4. It becomes a ticketwhere we already worked

A small build with a big payoff. Within two months we had feedback we couldn’t get any other way, at the moment of the action.

06 · Keeping the client

We kept the biggest client by shipping less, sooner.

One unsolved problem on the dashboard mattered to one client: Optio’s largest. They had explained it three times and were starting to sound like a customer with options.

  1. Months of askingThe client keeps raising the same problem.
  2. Interviews and testsWith their own participants. Direction agreed with engineering.
  3. “Pause”Other projects took the quarter. That pause was the real risk.
  4. Cut into slicesWith our Product Owner, shown at the quarterly OKR review.
  5. The client stayedThe new dashboard rolled out in pieces over the next quarters.

The idea: ship the parts that fixed their pain inside releases we’d already promised, and move the rest later. Leadership said yes, and the roadmap stayed honest.
