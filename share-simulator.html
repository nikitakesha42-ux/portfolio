Self-initiated rebuild · 2026 · working prototype

Rebuilding the share simulator around one question: what would I actually take home?

I designed Optio’s share simulator in 2022. It answered “what if the price moves?” with a slider and a total. People arrived with a different question, and often on their phone. So I rebuilt it from the smallest screen up: one sentence that answers the question, the source behind every number, and an AI co-pilot that explains the maths but never tells anyone what to do.

01 · The problem

The old simulator answered one question. People arrived with another.

At Optio, I designed the simulator participants used to see what their shares might be worth. You picked a multiple of today’s price on a slider and read two totals: current and simulated. It was accurate, and honest about being an estimate.

But it answered “what if the price moves?” while people were asking “what would I take home, and can I sell?” So they screenshotted numbers to compare next time, rebuilt the maths in Excel, and emailed HR to ask whether they were allowed to sell.

“A simulator that only returns a number isn’t simulating much.”

02 · Discovery

I started with what people do, not with what the programme can show.

The research lives in one Figma file and runs in a fixed order: observable behaviours, then the needs behind them, then How Might We statements, then design ideas for the one that mattered most. Each step only uses what the step before it found.

Four needs behind them

Need 01

Status you can act on

Know, line by line, what is unlocked, unlocking soon or locked, and whether you can act today. “Vested” is engineer language, not participant language.

Need 02

What needs me, and by when

See time-sensitive things before going looking for them: an unlock in 15 days, a sale pause next spring. Without that, people set their own reminders and still miss windows.

Need 03

A price with context

Today’s price means little without a baseline: what it was at your last visit, where it comes from, when it updates. Without it, people keep screenshots.

Need 04 · highest priority

My reality, not the programme’s

Your currency, your tax rule, what you actually receive. The old list mirrored the company’s data model, so people translated every number themselves.

The question I chose to answer

How might we reflect the participant’s reality, rather than internal programme state, so people feel informed instead of guessing?

I wrote 16 How Might We statements across the four needs, none with a solution baked in. This one went first because it sits under the other three: status, deadlines and price only help once they’re in your own currency and after your own tax.

Three ideas, each with a way to measure it

  • Lead with what you’d receive. Net after tax, in your currency, first. Grant details last. Measured by fewer “what’s my net?” questions to HR.
  • Name the action, not the lifecycle. “Can sell”, “Soon” and “Later” instead of “vested” and “unvested”. Measured in testing: can people point at a line they can act on today?
  • Show what changed since your last visit. A small change marker on the numbers that move. Measured by getting the same answer in one visit instead of three.
03 · People and journeys

Six people, not one “main user”.

I chose six personas along the lines where a design decision changes: how fluent someone is with finance, what’s happening in their life, which country’s rules apply, whether they use a screen reader, how they read and focus, and whether they’re helping someone else. If the page works for all six, it works for the people in between.

Three journeys, three emotional loads

Journey 01 · Curiosity

“I just got the letter”

Maren’s first visit, fifteen minutes between meetings. Low point: “Is this before or after tax? In NOK?” Opportunity: net after tax by default, and a countdown to the first unlock.

Journey 02 · Weighing

“Should I sell?”

Daniel, over four sessions in a week. Low point: tax shown as a percentage, not as kroner. Opportunity: selling now and waiting six months side by side, the difference in NOK, and a file for his accountant.

Journey 03 · Loss

“What do I keep if I leave?”

Priya, late at night, on her phone. Low point: she can’t see at a glance what she’d lose. Opportunity: an “if I leave” answer built from her own shares.

04 · Mobile first

I designed from the bottom up: phone first, laptop last.

Priya opens the simulator at 11 PM on her phone. Erik gives it two minutes between meetings. Fatima shares her laptop screen on a call. The smallest screen is the hardest one, so I started there.

A screen 402 pixels wide forces the decisions a big one lets you put off: one question per screen, one main action, every number labelled in words. A block that earned its place on the phone earned it everywhere. Then I turned the phone on its side, and only then opened a laptop. Each step rearranges the same blocks. Nothing new appears on the laptop and nothing is hidden on the phone, so someone who switches device finds the same page.

  • The price bar travels. Today’s price, its change, its source and what you can sell stay pinned at every size. It’s the anchor every other number hangs from.
  • Sized for thumbs first. Touch targets of at least 44 px, and form fields of at least 16 px so iOS never zooms the page when you tap one.
  • Landscape works, it isn’t blocked. Turning the phone gives the chart its own column instead of a “please rotate” message.
05 · Wireframe to prototype

Six wireframes, one wrong turn, four rounds of prototype.

First, the wrong turn

My first rebuild, in June, put a chat box at the centre: ask anything about your shares. The answers were good. But people had to know what to ask before they saw a single number, and Maren had already said what she wanted: a sentence she could repeat to the bank, not a conversation. The co-pilot stayed. It moved from the front door to a helper next to the numbers it explains.

Six low-fidelity screens, each with a reason

Back in Figma, every wireframe got a side note. It opens with the biggest usability problem the screen solves, then why and how, what it does for accessibility, what I considered and rejected, and the decision.

Two concepts on the phone

Before the prototype, I built two clickable concepts. A kept the old slider and made it fit a phone. B led with filters: type, plan, scenario. Both asked people to set things up before they saw their own number, which is the opposite of what the research asked for.

Four rounds on the prototype

  1. Round 1 · Straight from the wireframes. Everything on the first screen: a priority banner, the value, three status rows and two buttons. Faithful to the sketches, and too much at once.
  2. Round 2 · One question per screen. A stress test cut it back hard. Too hard: today’s price, its source and your position disappeared, and with them the context the research said people need.
  3. Round 3 · Anchors back. A price bar on every screen: today’s price, the change since your last visit, when it updated and from where, and how many shares you can sell.
  4. Round 4 · Details that change meaning. Spacing that groups by proximity, “blackout” replaced with “sale pause”, a four-step corner radius scale, one data source for every number, a real CSV download instead of a vague “share with HR”, and the laptop layout.
06 · Before and after

What changed, and why it matters.

From a multiplier to a sentence

The 2022 simulator asked you to think in multiples of the price and read two totals. The rebuild names the scenario in words and answers with what you’d take home, for which shares, in which year.

Simple, but not empty

Round 2 removed too much. Without today’s price and where it comes from, “4 250 NOK” is a number you can’t check. The price bar came back on every screen, and the sentence now says which shares the amount comes from.

Space does the grouping

The scenario chips sat right on top of the result, so choosing and reading blurred into one block and the labels clipped. Now there’s clear space between choosing and reading, and each chip has room for its price.

Words people already use

“No blackout risk”, “already vested” and “the window is open” assume you know the programme’s vocabulary. Now it says “No waiting, no company sale pause” and “already yours to sell”, and what a sale pause is sits one tap away.

From wireframe to working screen

The structure held from sketch to prototype: rows that lead with what you can do. What moved were the words, the anchors and the controls: grouping by year, plan or type, filters that match the status words, and a sentence when you open a row.

07 · The AI co-pilot

The co-pilot explains the maths. The decision stays with the person.

AI fluency · Automate

The maths runs itself

Tax rules, unlock dates, sale pauses and the price you pay for options, all worked out from your own programme. Every screen and every answer reads from one list, so no two numbers disagree.

Problem reframed · Cut

No front door that needs a question

Neither the slider nor the chat box opens the page any more. The answer does. The co-pilot sits one tap away, next to the number it explains: “Why?”

Risk mitigated · Keep human

Never “sell now”

It explains, compares and points to a tax advisor. It never recommends selling, holding or waiting. Wrong advice about someone’s own money is a risk not worth taking.

Built unprompted · Add with AI

Answers about your own shares

“What if I leave in 18 months?” is answered with your numbers: what unlocks, what goes back, and how long you have to use your options.

08 · Built for everyone

The accessible path is the default path.

Ola uses VoiceOver. Erik reads in short bursts. Priya holds the phone in one hand. None of them should need a special mode, so the accessible version is the only version.

WCAG 2.2 AA

A chart you can hear

The year handle is a real slider: arrow keys move it, and it announces the year. “Describe this chart” reads the whole trend as one sentence.

WCAG 4.1.3

Results that announce themselves

When you pick a price, the new take-home is read out through a polite live region. Nothing to go looking for.

WCAG 1.4.1

Never colour alone

Every status is a dot plus a word: “Can sell”, “Soon”, “Later”. Colour supports the meaning. It doesn’t carry it.

WCAG 2.5.8 · 1.3.4

Sized for thumbs, any way up

Touch targets of at least 44 px, fields that never trigger the iOS zoom, and a layout that works with the phone on its side.

Settings

Adjust on top of your phone

An Accessibility panel adds reduced motion, larger text and high contrast, plus a visible transcript of what a screen reader hears, so an HR partner can explain it on a call.

Plain language

One tap to the meaning

Jargon was replaced, not footnoted. Where a term has to stay, like “sale pause”, its meaning is one tap away, next to the price.

09 · Looking ahead

A working prototype, not a shipped product. Here’s how I’d know it works.

None of this has been tested with real participants yet. The next step is a moderated round with six people across the personas, including a VoiceOver user, measured against what the design ideas already defined.

Measure 01

Fewer “can I sell?” questions

HR tickets phrased as “can I sell?” or “what’s my net?”, before and after.

Measure 02

Faster to the first useful action

Time from opening the page to choosing a scenario or opening a row.

Measure 03

One visit instead of three

Visits per person per week for the same information, and fewer people asking “did this change since last time?”

Three things I’d tell myself at the start

Lesson 01

Simplify toward the question, not away from the facts

Round 2 looked clean and worked worse. Taking away the price and its source took away the trust.

Lesson 02

Words are part of the interface

Swapping “blackout” for “sale pause” was a one-word change that made the rule readable to everyone, not just people who already knew it.

Lesson 03

Start small and the big screen gets easy

Every hard choice happened at 402 pixels. The laptop layout only had to rearrange blocks that had already earned their place.
