01 · The problem

My old simulator answered a question people weren’t asking.

At Optio, I designed the page people used to see what their company shares might be worth. You moved a slider to pick a price, like “five times today’s”, and read two totals. It was correct, and honest about being a guess.

But people didn’t come to ask “what if the price moves?” They came to ask “what would I take home, and can I sell?”

So they found their own ways. They took screenshots to compare next time. They redid the maths in Excel. They emailed HR and waited for an answer.

“A simulator that only returns a number isn’t simulating much.”

02 · What people needed

People wanted their own reality, not the programme’s.

I started with what people did when the simulator let them down, and asked what they were really missing. Some of it was about status, deadlines and a price they could trust. But one need sat under all the others.

Your currency. Your tax. What actually lands in your bank account. The old page showed the company’s data, so people had to translate every number themselves. Nothing else helps until the numbers are in your own money.

How do I show people their own reality, so they feel informed instead of guessing?

Three rules for every screen

  • Lead with what you’d receive. Money after tax, in your currency, comes first. The fine print comes last.
  • Say what you can do. “Can sell”, “Soon” and “Later” instead of “vested” and “unvested”. You should be able to point at what you can act on today.
  • Show what changed. A small marker on the numbers that moved since your last visit, so nobody needs a screenshot to remember.
03 · The wrong turn

My first idea was a chat box. It was the wrong front door.

In June I put a chat box in the middle of the page: ask anything about your shares. The answers were good. But there was a catch.

You had to know what to ask before you saw a single number. And Maren had already told me what she wanted: a sentence for the bank, not a conversation.

So the chat didn’t disappear. It moved, from the front door to a helper standing next to the number it explains.

Two more tries, the same mistake

Next I built two clickable ideas. One kept the old slider. The other started with filters. Both made people set things up before they saw their own number. That told me what the page had to do: show the answer first.

04 · Phone first

I started on the smallest screen, because that’s where people ask.

Priya checks her shares late at night on her phone. Others give it two minutes between meetings. So the phone came first.

A small screen won’t let you put off hard choices. One question per screen. One main button. Every number explained in words. Then I turned the phone on its side, and only then opened a laptop. The laptop gets more room, not more stuff, so you find the same page on every device.

05 · Finding the balance

First I showed too much. Then too little. Then I found the middle.

My first working version put everything on the first screen. It matched my sketches, and it was too much at once.

So I cut hard. It looked clean. It worked worse. Today’s price was gone, and so was where it came from. Without them, the amount on screen was a number nobody could check. And a number you can’t check is a number you don’t trust.

So the anchors came back: a price bar on every screen, with today’s price, what changed since your last visit, where the price comes from and what you can sell.

Simple, but not empty

The too-simple version said “4 250 NOK” with nothing to check it against. Now the price and its source sit on top, and the sentence says which shares the money comes from.

From a slider to a sentence

The old page made you think in multiples of the price and read two totals. The new one names the choice in words and answers with what you’d take home, for which shares, in which year.

Words people already use

“No blackout risk” and “already vested” only make sense if you know the programme’s words. Now it says “No waiting, no company sale pause” and “already yours to sell”.

06 · The AI co-pilot

The co-pilot explains the maths. The decision stays with you.

What it does

The maths runs itself

Tax, unlock dates and sale pauses are worked out from your own shares. Every screen and every answer reads from the same list, so no two numbers disagree.

Where it sits

Next to the number, not in front of it

The answer opens the page. The co-pilot waits one tap away, next to the number it explains: “Why?”

What it never does

Never “sell now”

It explains, compares and points to a tax advisor. It never says sell, hold or wait. Wrong advice about someone’s own money is a risk not worth taking.

What it can answer

Questions about your own shares

“What if I leave?” gets an answer with your numbers: what unlocks, what goes back, and how long you have to use your options.

07 · Built for everyone

The accessible way is the only way.

Ola uses VoiceOver. Priya holds the phone in one hand. Neither of them should need a special mode, so there is only one version, and it works for both.

Screen readers

A chart you can hear

Arrow keys move through the years, and each year is read out. Pick a price and the new amount is read out too. “Describe this chart” tells the whole story in one sentence.

Colour

Never colour alone

Every status is a dot plus a word: “Can sell”, “Soon”, “Later”. Colour helps. The word carries the meaning.

Touch

Made for one thumb

Big buttons, no surprise zoom when you tap a field, and a page that still works with the phone on its side.

Plain language

One tap to the meaning

Jargon was replaced, not footnoted. Where a word has to stay, like “sale pause”, its meaning is one tap away, next to the price.

08 · Looking ahead

It works, but no real user has tried it yet. Here’s how I’d know it’s right.

Next, I’d sit with people like Maren, Daniel, Priya and Ola, including someone who uses VoiceOver, and watch them find their answer.

Sign it works

Fewer “can I sell?” emails to HR

People find the answer on the page instead of waiting for a reply.

Sign it works

A quick first step

People pick a price or open a row soon after they arrive, without hunting.

Sign it works

One visit is enough

People stop coming back to check the same thing, and stop asking “did this change since last time?”

What I’d tell myself at the start

Lesson

Simplify toward the question, not away from the facts

The too-simple version looked clean and worked worse. When the price and its source went, the trust went with them.

Lesson

Words are part of the design

Swapping “blackout” for “sale pause” was one word. It made the rule clear to everyone, not just people who already knew it.

Lesson

Start small and the big screen gets easy

Every hard choice happened on the phone. The laptop only had to rearrange pieces that had already earned their place.
