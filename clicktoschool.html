01Overview

Project
ClickToSchool
My role
Product Designer (volunteer) Accessibility lead
Year
2020

Problem

The site reached a wide audience — including people who don't usually use donation flows. The existing experience felt generic and transactional, and didn't help first-time visitors feel safe taking that first step.

How I framed it

With no research budget and a volunteer team, the design had to do the work that paid testing usually would. I framed the project around a single principle: the visitor must feel the project is real, the people behind it are real, and their contribution will land somewhere specific. Everything else — copy, layout, spacing, accessibility — followed from that. If a design choice didn't help with at least one of those three, it was cut.

Goal

  • Make donating feel safe, welcoming and easy to understand.
  • Build accessibility in from the first sketch — not as a checklist at the end.
  • Make the project something supporters feel comfortable sharing.

02Core Challenges

  • A wide audience meant no single mental model — copy and visual language had to land across age, tech comfort and language.
  • Volunteer team, no dedicated research budget — decisions leaned on heuristics and lightweight testing.
  • Trust matters a lot in charity contexts — visual care reads as credibility, not polish for its own sake.

03Key Solutions

Plain language, calm layout

The content was rewritten alongside the design — moving away from donor-platform jargon and toward words closer to how people actually talk about giving. Generous spacing and warm typography were deliberate: the previous version pressed visitors with stacked CTAs, and the new layout gave the project space to introduce itself before asking for anything.

Accessibility as a starting point

The audience for a charity site spans every demographic, including older visitors, screen-reader users, and people on slow connections in shared devices. Designing for that audience second is designing for the average user — and the average user doesn't exist here. Colour contrast, focus states, reading order and keyboard flows were defined in the very first mockups, so they couldn't be quietly traded away when the team was tired.

A friendly, step-by-step donation flow

A donation flow has to do two contradictory things at once: feel quick enough that motivated donors don't drop off, and feel calm enough that hesitant donors don't bail. I split the flow into short, ordered steps where each step states what's happening and what comes next. The friction is intentional and small — just enough that nobody arrives at the confirmation screen wondering what they just signed up for.

04Outcomes

  • Supporters started actively sharing the site — the kind of word-of-mouth growth that's hard to design for, but easy to spot when it happens.
  • Accessible-by-default patterns became the baseline for later volunteer redesigns.
  • A non-commercial brief that reminds me emotional honesty and product clarity work better together than apart.
